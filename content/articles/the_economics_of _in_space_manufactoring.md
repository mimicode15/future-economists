What if the next breakthrough cancer drug or ultra-fast internet cable was manufactured 250 miles above your head, in orbit?

When you think about building the factories to do so in space, it can easily sound like a science-fiction novel; launching a singular rocket now still costs millions of dollars. Since the beginning of space exploration, orbit has been treated as a place for astronauts and researchers to run government-funded and managed science experiments, not a practical or useful manufacturing field for the commercial supply chain.

Even now, to picture this change means reframing our view of space and orbit as a distant destination to seeing it as an opportunity; a unique industrial environment not unlike a vacuum chamber or ultra-cooled sanitary room. With this, the business and economic math begins to shift entirely. In the third part of this Space Economy series, we’ll explain exactly why weightlessness creates superior and cutting-edge materials, what companies are actually producing in orbit, how automated capsules are working without human crews, and the strict, key price-per-kilogram formula that determines what gets made in space. Now that falling launch prices are turning low Earth orbit into a fascinating and possibly crucial extension of Earth’s high tech manufacturing floor, having a deep economic understanding of the mechanics behind this shift is critical.

## Why Weightlessness Makes Better Products

On Earth, gravity is the glue that keeps us on our feet. In Earth’s factories, however, it can create microscopic flaws in medicines, glass fibers, and computer chips. But why is this, and is manufacturing in low-Earth orbit the solution?

When a company launches a factory into low-Earth orbit, it enters microgravity, or a state of near-weightlessness. With this removal of gravity’s constant pull, three problems that affect Earth’s factories are eliminated as well. The first of these is sedimentation, a process in which suspended solid particles settle out of a fluid and collect at the bottom due to gravity or other forces based on their weight. In other words, the conditions of a space factory erase the sinking and floating of particles; ingredients stay evenly suspended in place regardless of earthly weight, making it possible to blend ultra-pure metal alloys and uniform medicines. The second issue resolved is convection (the transfer of heat or thermal energy through the physical movement of a fluid). Warm air and liquid (steam) rise naturally, while their cooler counterparts sink. This constant mixing stirs liquids as they cool and harden, knocking delicate molecules out of alignment. In orbit, heated liquids stay still, allowing crystals and glass fibers to grow slowly and evenly with no fear of structural cracks. Lastly, there is the problem of container contamination. Melted materials must sit in solid molds or dishes, which touch the liquid and can leave behind microscopic impurities. Yet due to microgravity, surface tension pulls floating liquids into smooth, self-contained spheres, effectively getting rid of impurities left behind by containers.

Essentially, LEO (low-Earth orbit) factories act as highly sanitized, unmatched clean rooms, allowing for the production of stronger, purer materials that no current factory on Earth can replicate.

## What Companies Are Actually Making in Space

Even with the falling launch costs and heavy investment into space companies dedicated to the purpose of in-orbit manufacturing, the overall costs of getting the factory into orbit and routinely shipping cargo to and from it remain extremely expensive. This leaves the question: what exactly is worth producing in space that cannot be created on Earth as effectively?

Overall, the goods that will be created in space will share this theme: they will be very high value goods where even a tiny quantity is worth millions.

Currently, three big industries fit that criteria. Precision pharmaceuticals, ultra-clear optical fibers, and next-generation semiconductors are all produced in monitored, delicate environments and sold for high amounts.

**Precision Pharmaceuticals:** a tiny, lightweight dose of a highly-demanded medicine holds high value. And in microgravity, drug molecules form larger, more uniform crystals than they do on Earth. Companies like <a href="https://www.varda.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Varda Space Industries</a> have already produced crystals of the antiviral drug ritonavir (used as a pharmacokinetic "booster" in combination with other medications to treat mainly HIV/AIDS and COVID-19) in orbiting capsules and returned them to Earth safely and intact. The pharmaceutical giant <a href="https://finance.yahoo.com/quote/MRK" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Merck</a> has used space experiments to study and improve how its cancer drug Keytruda is structured, with the purpose of turning long hospital IV drips into a simple, swift injection.

**Ultra-Clear Optical Fibers:** Over long distances, standard glass internet cables lose signal strength, often due to gravity causing microscopic crystal flaws in the glass as it cools. Aboard the International Space Station (ISS), companies like <a href="https://finance.yahoo.com/quote/RDW" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Redwire</a> produce a specialized fluoride glass called ZBLAN. Without gravity to pull at the cooling glass, ZBLAN fibers transmit light with much less signal loss than the standard cables, making them highly valuable for undersea communications and precision medical lasers.

**Next-Generation Semiconductors:** Every modern computer chip contains ultra-pure crystal wafers, invaluable for transmitting electricity quickly without overheating. Startups like the UK-based <a href="https://www.spaceforge.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Space Forge</a> are building returnable in-orbit platforms to create defect-free semiconductor crystals in space. Even a tiny batch of these space-grown “seed” crystals can be brought back to Earth and used to manufacture faster, cooler, and more energy-efficient chips that are key for building AI data centers and electric vehicles.

## Autonomous Capsules vs. Crewed Stations

In the not-so-distant past, manufacturing products in orbit meant sending up equipment to the ISS where it was managed by astronauts, which could be a costly and potentially life-threatening situation. Now, as private companies look to turn LEO into a tangible, thriving commercial supply chain, that business model is splitting into two options.

While crewed space stations remain the ideal for intricate, early-stage research in which human hands and brains are needed to adjust fragile tools, swap out test samples, or fix unexpected glitches on the spot, keeping astronauts alive in orbit requires crucial and massive life-support systems, strict safety margins, and expensive resupply missions, all costs that eat into commercial profit.

To mitigate this running expense, a new wave of startups is focusing on the development of uncrewed return capsules. Varda Space Industries, for example, is launching compact, automated mini-factories that can orbit Earth without onboard assistance for weeks at a time. How does this process work? Once onboard machines finish growing drug crystals or processing materials in microgravity, the capsule separates and drops back into the atmosphere, landing with a parachute for swift recovery. With the removal of human crews and their costs, these capsules cut operating costs, dodge astronaut safety delays, and allow companies to run faster, repeatable production cycles.

## The Price-Per-Kilogram Math, And Why It’s So Important

How can these companies be sure that they will make a profit from their immense efforts in space manufacturing? All else equal, it comes down to one thing: the math must pass a strict test. The finished product's price per kilogram must outweigh the combined cost of launching raw materials, powering automated machines in orbit, and safely returning the precious cargo to Earth.

Right now, only a few ultra-high value products clear that bar. Yet as reusable heavy-lift rockets lower launch costs yearly, that break-even threshold will steadily drop, transforming LEO into a profitable, productive, and invaluable link in the global supply chain.
